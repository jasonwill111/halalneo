# 04 — Packaging & Labeling Requirements for Halal Food Products

**Research ticket:** Halal trade B2B knowledge base — packaging and labeling requirements for halal food products.
**Status:** Research complete · **Sources:** high-trust regulators, Codex Alimentarius, OIC/SMIIC, certifying-body guides (see Sources).

---

## Executive Summary

Halal product compliance is governed by two overlapping layers: **general food labeling law** (baseline requirements for any food product, codified internationally by the Codex Alimentarius *General Standard for the Labelling of Prepackaged Foods*, CXS 1-1985) and **halal-specific rules** (the Codex *General Guidelines for Use of the Term "Halal"*, CAC/GL 24-1997, plus national/importing-market regulations). Any halal-labeled product must satisfy both layers simultaneously — a product can be perfectly halal yet still be rejected for a missing ingredient declaration, and a product with perfect general labeling can fail for an unauthorized halal mark.

Key takeaways for HalalNeo:

1. **Mandatory label elements** (Codex CXS 1-1985): name of the food, list of ingredients in descending order, allergen declaration, net contents/drained weight, manufacturer/packer name & address, country of origin, lot identification, date marking + storage conditions, and instructions for use. Ingredients, allergens, and lot codes are the three most commonly flagged in import audits.
2. **Halal claims vs. halal logos**: The term "halal" alone is a *claim* that must be truthful and substantiated. An **official halal logo** is a regulated mark owned by a specific certifying body (e.g., JAKIM, IFANCA, ISNA/CHART), whose use is licensed per-product under a certification agreement — it is *not* free for use. Several jurisdictions legally require the certifier's full name next to any halal claim (e.g., Canada CFIA B.01.050; USDA FSIS for meat/poultry; US state laws in NY, IL, NJ, CA, TX).
3. **Packaging** must be free of haram/najis components: no pig-derived materials, no non-halal animal derivatives (gelatin, tallow, lard) in inks, coatings, adhesives, or substrates; residual alcohol must evaporate; and halal vs. non-halal production must be segregated to prevent cross-contamination (SMIIC 1, GSO 2652:2021, AHF).
4. **Most common audit pitfalls**: missing/inconsistent ingredient declarations, translation errors (esp. Arabic for Gulf markets), unsupported claims, date-format issues, barcode/document mismatches, and misuse or misplacement of halal logos. Label problems alone can trigger customs holds or rejection even when the product itself is compliant.

## 1. Mandatory Label Elements for Food Products (Codex / Common Market Rules)

The global baseline is **Codex CXS 1-1985, *General Standard for the Labelling of Prepackaged Foods***, which most national food laws implement. Codex Section 4 ("Mandatory Labelling of Prepackaged Foods") requires the following, as applicable:

| Element | Codex requirement | Why it matters in trade |
|---|---|---|
| **Name of the food** | Specific, true name (Codex name, or national-legislation name, or common/usual name); brand/trade names are additional, not a substitute. | Misleading/generic names trigger scrutiny (Codex §4.1). |
| **List of ingredients** | All ingredients in **descending order of ingoing weight**; compound ingredients declared with their sub-ingredients; food additives named (class + specific name or recognized code). | The single most common label-compliance failure in import audits. |
| **Allergens** | Foods/ingredients known to cause hypersensitivity (e.g., gluten, milk, eggs, fish, crustacea, tree nuts, peanuts, soy, sesame, sulfites) "shall always be declared"; allergens from GM/biotech transfer must be declared. | Undeclared allergens are a leading cause of recalls, detentions, and rejections. |
| **Net contents & drained weight** | Metric units; liquids by volume, solids by weight; drained weight for food packed in liquid medium. | Incorrect units/formatting = inspection flag. |
| **Name & address** | Manufacturer, packer, distributor, importer, exporter, or vendor. | Incomplete/outdated contact info is a frequent non-compliance. |
| **Country of origin** | Declared if omission would mislead; for foods processed in a second country, the processing country is the origin. | Required for customs + consumer transparency. |
| **Lot identification** | Each container permanently marked (code or clear) identifying producing factory and lot. | Core of traceability; required for recall and customs verification. |
| **Date marking** | "Date of minimum durability" (best-before) at minimum; storage conditions declared if validity depends on them. | Date-format and consistency issues cause many holds. |
| **Instructions for use** | Included where necessary for correct utilization/reconstitution. | Required for many processed products. |

**Additional Codex requirements** (Section 5): **quantitative ingredient labelling** when a label emphasizes a characterizing ingredient; **irradiation declaration** (any food treated with ionizing radiation must state so near the name of the food; irradiated ingredients must be declared in the ingredient list). **Presentation rules** (Section 8): labels must be clear, prominent, indelible, legible, not separated from the container, and name + net contents must appear in the same field of vision. **Language**: a supplementary label may carry mandatory information in the consumer's language; relabeling/supplementary labels must accurately reflect the original. Small units with largest surface area <10 cm² are exempt from ingredient and date/instructions requirements (except spices/herbs).

National laws layer specifics onto this baseline — e.g., the **US** emphasizes allergen + nutrition-facts labeling; the **EU** requires strict ingredient transparency and multilingual labels; **Asia-Pacific** markets vary in language, format, and importer-responsibility rules. For halal trade specifically, the importing country's rules (e.g., GCC/SFDA, Malaysia/JAKIM, Indonesia/BPJPH, UAE) govern the final label, and these are frequently stricter than the exporter's domestic rules.

## 2. Halal Logo / Mark Usage Rules

### 2.1 Regulated vs. unregulated "halal"

- **"Halal" as a claim** is, in most markets, an unregulated-to-loosely-regulated *assertion*: under Codex **CAC/GL 24-1997**, the term may be used when the food meets the halal definition (no unlawful component, no contact with unlawful food during preparation/processing/transport/storage, prepared with facilities free of unlawful material, and compliant hygiene). The **FDA** does not define or regulate "halal" (separation of church and state); it treats halal as a religious dietary claim subject to general truthfulness rules.
- **An official halal logo is a regulated mark**, not a generic phrase. It is a proprietary trademark owned by a **certifying body** (e.g., JAKIM in Malaysia, IFANCA/AHF in the US, ISNA Halal under CHART in Canada, GİMDES in Türkiye) and is licensed for use on specific certified products. Conditions of use are contractually controlled by the issuing body, which specifies artwork, sizing, placement, renewal (typically 1–3 years), and audit obligations. Using a certifier's logo without authorization is misrepresentation, counterfeiting, or dilution of the mark (e.g., TÜV SÜD's "Procedure for Use of Halal Certification Mark" defines improper use as infringement, imitation, counterfeiting, or dilution).

### 2.2 Jurisdictions that regulate halal claims and logos

- **Canada (CFIA)**: Under the *Food and Drug Regulations* (B.01.050), a food cannot be represented as halal unless **the name of the person or body that certified it** is shown on the label/package or in the advertisement/sale. CFIA guidance: acronyms/logos alone "may not be recognized," so the **complete certifier name** must appear wherever the halal claim is made. "Halal-style" wording that creates an impression of halal is treated as a halal representation.
- **USA — USDA FSIS**: For meat/poultry, the terms "Halal"/"Zabiah Halal" must be certified by an appropriate third-party authority; FSIS oversees this. **State laws** criminalize fraud: Illinois *Halal Food Act* (misrepresentation = Class B misdemeanor), New York *Halal Foods Protection Act* (registration with Dept. of Agriculture), New Jersey *Halal Food Consumer Protection Law* (labeling + recordkeeping), California Penal Code 383c (misdemeanor to defraud on halal), Texas truth-in-labeling.
- **Importing countries (OIC/GCC)**: Most OIC markets require a recognized certifying body's certificate + logo and may require the certifier to be accredited (e.g., by SMIIC) and mutually recognized; certificates from exporting-country religious authorities "should be accepted in principle" by importing countries under Codex GL 24, subject to national rules.

### 2.3 Conditions of use / best practice

- Display the certifying body's official logo **only on the certified product line** and only with a current, in-scope certificate.
- Pair any halal wordmark/symbol with the certifier's **full legal name** on the label and in advertising.
- Keep certificates valid and renewed (1–3 years typical), and ensure the product scope on the certificate matches the product on the label.
- Do not use a logo to imply certification that was not granted, or extend a certified logo to non-certified variants/plants.

## 3. Halal Packaging Material Considerations

Packaging is part of the halal status: under Codex GL 24, halal food must not be "in direct contact with any food that fails" the halal test, and must be "prepared, processed, packaged, transported and stored" in a manner consistent with halal requirements and Codex hygiene principles. **SMIIC 1 (OIC) "General Requirements for Halal Food"** (and GCC-standard **GSO 2652:2021** on halal packaging) require packaging materials not to be made from non-halal materials and halal foodstuffs to be identifiable throughout handling. Key requirements:

1. **No haram/najis components in any packaging material.** Packaging substrates (plastics, paper, cardboard, glass, metal foils) are generally acceptable, but animal-derived components are a risk: **gelatin/collagen** in glues/adhesives, **tallow or lard** in lubricants and additives, **bone charcoal** in filters, animal-based **stearates, glycerins, pigments, shellac** in inks/coatings — all haram unless sourced from halal-slaughtered animals.
2. **Inks, coatings, and adhesives.** Print inks and varnishes can carry non-halal components; alcohol-based inks/adhesives/solvents (ethanol, isopropyl alcohol) must evaporate completely with no residual alcohol. Soy-based inks and water-based coatings are recommended as safer choices. Printing/ink contamination of halal base packaging material is a documented certification risk.
3. **Cross-contamination control.** Halal packaging operations must be segregated from haram/conventional runs (dedicated lines or rigorous cleaning/SOPs between runs); shared equipment requires flushing/purging and sanitation free of najis (pork residue, blood). Facilities should establish "halal zones" and halal-critical control points (HCCPs) for materials and processes.
4. **Recycled & bio-based materials.** Recycled plastics/papers are acceptable only if the recycling stream is known to be halal-compatible and cleaned; bioplastics/cellulose films are generally halal if processing aids comply.
5. **Certification of packaging itself.** Many halal product manufacturers now require halal-certified packaging from suppliers (document audit → facility audit → certification; renewal 1–3 years). Documentation includes raw-material lists, ingredient specs, process flow diagrams, sanitization procedures, and a halal policy manual.

## 4. Common Label Audit Pitfalls (Rejected Imports / Compliance Failures)

Based on customs-detention data and certifier/regulator guidance, the most frequent failures are:

1. **Incomplete or inconsistent ingredient declarations.** Missing ingredient lists, wrong descending-order sequencing, incomplete additive info, and mismatches between the label and technical specs/certificates are the top rejection triggers (FDA detentions, SFDA clarification requests, CFIA).
2. **Undeclared or poorly formatted allergens.** Allergen omissions or inconsistent formatting lead to recalls/detentions; allergens must be declared per the importing market's list and format.
3. **Halal logo misuse / incomplete certification info.** Using only a logo without the certifier's full name (Canada); using a logo without a valid in-scope certificate; expired or unrecognized certificates; counterfeit or vague logos. Missing/expired halal certificate, or certificate mismatched to the invoice, is a leading documentation risk in halal trade.
4. **Translation and language failures.** Incorrect ingredient translations, misleading terminology, missing mandatory information in the required language (e.g., Arabic for Gulf markets — the SFDA rejects labels lacking required Arabic: product name, ingredients, net quantity, storage, origin, manufacturer, expiry, usage). Translation must be treated as regulatory compliance, not marketing.
5. **Date-marking and shelf-life errors.** Unclear date formats, production/expiry inconsistencies with supporting documents, stickers covering original dates.
6. **Unsupported claims.** Health/nutritional/functional claims, and religious claims, require substantiation or certification; unsupported claims attract regulatory review and can void an otherwise valid halal status in the consumer's/regulator's eyes.
7. **Documentation mismatches.** Barcode/product-code mismatches vs. registration files, ingredient lists differing across documents, and invoices not matching halal certificates cause customs holds even for compliant products.
8. **Domestic-label reuse across markets.** A label compliant in one country often fails in another; pre-shipment label review per target market (translation validation, ingredient verification, claim assessment, shelf-life review, cross-check against registration docs) is the standard mitigation.
