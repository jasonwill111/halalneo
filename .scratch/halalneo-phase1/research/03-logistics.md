# Halal Logistics & Supply Chain Requirements

**Research ticket:** HalalNeo phase-1 - logistics & supply chain
**Status:** Complete
**Date:** 2026-08-10

---

## Executive Summary

Halal logistics is the discipline of keeping a product's halal status intact through every movement, storage, and handling step between the certified production site and the end consumer, commonly described as "from farm to fork." A product made perfectly halal can lose its halal integrity (and its certification) if it is transported, warehoused, or consolidated with non-halal or najis (ritually unclean) goods without proper controls.

The regulatory landscape is converging on auditable management-system standards rather than mere product labelling:

- **Malaysia** - **MS 2400:2019** _Halal supply chain management system_, a three-part standard (Part 1 Transportation, Part 2 Warehousing, Part 3 Retailing) enforced through **JAKIM** logistics certification and the **Malaysian Halal Management System (MHMS 2020)**. Certified in practice by JAKIM-audited logistics providers.
- **Indonesia** - **HAS 23000-5** _Halal Certification Requirements for the Logistic Industry_ (LPPOM MUI scheme) plus a BPJPH regulatory regime (PP 39/2021 and a draft BPJPH logistics decree notified to the WTO TBT in Nov 2025) that makes **halal certification of third-party logistics services mandatory** for food, beverage, pharma and cosmetics, phased in from Oct 2024 through Oct 2026.
- **International** - **OIC/SMIIC 17:2020** (SMIIC Technical Committee, Turkiye) is the international counterpart of MS 2400 (Transportation, Warehousing, Retailing, plus a Port module under drafting); **GSO OIC/SMIIC 17-1/17-2:2024** are the Gulf adoptions. **ISO 31512:2024** covers B2B cold-chain storage and transport. "**GPAI**" is not a certification standard in the sources reviewed; the term most plausibly refers to the **Global Programme for Halal Industry** (an HDC/Malaysia industry-development and training programme) - the acronym should be verified with the requester (see section 1.4).

Core operational principles, consistent across all these instruments:

1. **Segregation** - physical and demonstrable separation of halal from non-halal / najis goods during storage, transport and terminal handling (dedicated facilities/zones, containers, pallets, handling equipment, colour-coding).
2. **Contamination avoidance** - cleaning regimes including ritual cleansing (**sertu/samak**), seals, drip prevention, stacking rules (halal above non-halal), and personnel hygiene.
3. **Traceability and chain of custody** - documentation at every handover so auditors and consumers can verify halal status from origin to destination.
4. **Temperature control** - for perishables, a monitored cold chain (chilled 0-5 C, frozen -18 C or below).

**Key takeaway for HalalNeo:** halal trade is increasingly a supply-chain obligation, not just a product label. Buyers in Malaysia, Indonesia, the GCC and beyond will expect proof that the logistics leg (3PL, cold chain, port/airport handling, last-mile) is itself halal-certified or follows a documented segregation/custody protocol. The knowledge base should present halal logistics as a distinct certification scope (facility + process + people), not an afterthought to product certification.

## 1. Halal Logistics Certification and Standards

### 1.1 What "halal logistics" means in practice

Halal logistics is the process of managing procurement, movement, storage and handling of materials and (semi-)finished goods, both food and non-food, in compliance with the general principles of Shariah law. Its objective is to secure the **halal integrity** of products for the end consumer: "the command and control of goods flows in a value system in such a way that the halal integrity is secured throughout the supply chain" (Halal Malaysia / IHIAS 0100; Marco Tieman). Three fundamentals recur in the literature:

1. **Avoid direct contact with haram (cross-contamination).** Halal and non-halal goods are not mixed on handling equipment, pallets, load carriers, containers or in bulk shipments.
2. **Address contamination risk based on product characteristics** (bulk vs unitised, dry vs wet, ambient vs chilled/frozen).
3. **Address consumer perception** - the Muslim consumer's requirements shaped by school of law, local fatwas and customs (JEEIR; Tieman).

Halal logistics goes beyond food safety: it adds religious-integrity controls on top of HACCP/ISO 22000 hygiene. Non-Muslim-country providers can comply by segregation, cleaning/disinfection between loads, and dedicated or dedicated-use equipment and personnel (Memet, "Aspects to be known by marine carriers").

### 1.2 MS 2400 (Malaysia)

**MS 2400:2019** _Halal supply chain management system_ (Department of Standards Malaysia / JSM, confirmed 2024) is the principal reference, in three parts:

- **MS 2400-1:2019 - Transportation - General requirements.** Covers assurance of halal integrity of goods/cargo handled through all modes of transport. Key clauses: Shariah requirements, management responsibility, Halal Management System (HMS), halal control points, and "general requirements for premises, infrastructure, facilities and personnel."
- **MS 2400-2:2019 - Warehousing - General requirements.** Covers assurance of halal integrity at the warehouse, from receiving to delivery.
- **MS 2400-3:2019 - Retailing - General requirements.** Covers the retail stage (the final touchpoint with the consumer).

Certification pathway in Malaysia: JAKIM is the certifying authority; the Malaysia Halal Management System (MHMS 2020) defines the Halal Assurance System (HAS, for large/medium operators) and Internal Halal Control System (IHCS, for small/micro operators). A **Logistics Scheme (Skim Logistik)** exists under MHMS 2020 requiring: a halal policy, a Halal Management Committee and appointed Halal Executive, risk-control procedures for warehousing/retailing activities (cross-docking, break-bulk, consolidation, third-party contracts), traceability procedures, internal halal audits (min. once a year), and management review. Operators handling halal goods are encouraged to use halal-certified logistics providers; preferred-supplier clauses appear in certification checklists.

The MS 2400 series was first published in 2010 as _Halalan-Toyyiban Assurance Pipeline_ and revised in 2019 (title change to _Halal supply chain management system_). MS 2400 is widely cited as a benchmark that influenced standards in other jurisdictions (SMIIC, GSO, Indonesia's draft decree).

### 1.3 HAS 23000 and Indonesia's regulatory regime

- **HAS 23000-5** _Halal Certification Requirements for the Logistic Industry_ (LPPOM MUI) is the Indonesian certification scheme for halal logistics. Scope: warehousing, transportation, distribution and production-facility services that handle food, beverages, medicines and cosmetics. The system is called SJPH (Sistem Jaminan Produk Halal / Halal Product Assurance System) under the new regulatory framework, replacing the older SJH.
- **Mandatory certification.** Under **Law 33/2014** and **Government Regulation PP 39/2021** (as amended by PP 42/2024), halal certification became mandatory for food/beverage products from **17 Oct 2024**, extending to most food and beverage products by **17 Oct 2026**, with meat and dairy already mandatory. Seven service categories carry the halal obligation: slaughtering, processing, storage, packaging, distribution, sales and serving - but **only when serving food, beverages, drugs and cosmetics**. BPJPH (Badan Penyelenggara Jaminan Produk Halal) clarified (Sep 2024) that it is the **logistics service (jasa logistik) that must be certified, not the vehicle/truck itself**. Third-party logistics providers serving halal products must be certified; manufacturers using their own in-house storage/distribution are covered by their product certification.
- **Distribution rules (PP 42/2024 Article 22).** Halal and non-halal/non-certified products may share a distribution facility only if: (1) the goods are **not fresh or frozen processed products of animal origin** (those require fully separate facilities); and (2) cross-contamination is prevented, evidenced by a written declaration from the manufacturer or distributor.
- **Draft BPJPH decree (2025).** A draft decree "Guidelines for the Implementation of the Halal Product Assurance System for Storage, Packaging and Distribution Services" was notified to the WTO TBT (G/TBT/N/IDN/183, Nov 2025). It would require halal labelling on depot walls/pillars and vehicle bodies/doors, segregation of halal/non-halal goods, define halal critical points at seaports, airports and distribution centres, and harmonise with SMIIC 17, MS 2400, HAS 23000-5, HACCP/ISO 22000 and MUI fatwas. Sea and air freight operators would be in scope.

### 1.4 International harmonisation: OIC/SMIIC 17, GSO, and the "GPAI" question

- **OIC/SMIIC 17:2020** _Halal supply chain management system_ (Standards and Metrology Institute for the Islamic Countries, Turkiye) mirrors MS 2400 with three parts (Transportation, Warehousing, Retailing). SMIIC TC 10 is drafting a fourth **Port** module.
- **GSO OIC/SMIIC 17-1:2024** (Transportation) and **17-2:2024** (Warehousing) are the GCC adoptions published by the GSO standards store, extending halal supply-chain requirements to Gulf markets.
- **ISO 31512:2024** _Cold chain logistics services in the B2B sector - Requirements and guidelines for storage and transport_ provides the conventional temperature-control baseline that halal cold chains build on.
- **"GPAI"** - The web sources reviewed surfaced no halal logistics certification standard by this acronym. The acronym most plausibly denotes the **Global Programme for Halal Industry**, an industry-development/capacity-building programme associated with Malaysia's Halal Development Corporation (HDC) ecosystem rather than an auditable standard. It is recommended the requester confirm whether GPAI refers to that programme, to the Global Partnership on Artificial Intelligence (unrelated), or to something else. The auditable reference standards remain MS 2400, HAS 23000-5, OIC/SMIIC 17 and GSO adoptions.

### 1.5 What a halal logistics certificate actually covers

Halal logistics certification certifies the **facility and its processes**, not a product. Typical scope (American Halal Foundation; HalalExpo; LPPOM MUI):

- **Halal Assurance System / policy** - written halal policy, appointed halal officer/team, training records (halal awareness + role-specific; internal training at least annually).
- **Handling practices** - physical or temporal segregation, clear halal identification on units/packages, tamper-evident seals.
- **Vehicles & containers** - records confirming no prior najis/haram cargo or complete cleansing; halal-dedicated vehicles preferred; halal-approved cleaning materials (no alcohol); validated cold-chain documentation for chilled/frozen.
- **Warehousing** - separate storage, halal-compliant cleaning SOPs, halal-approved pest control, documented segregation.
- **Traceability & documentation** - invoices, B/L, AWB and packing lists indicating halal status; batch/cargo ID traceability shipper-to-receiver.
- **Audits** - internal audits at least every 6 months (CEROL v3.0 criteria), annual management review, and surveillance by the certification body.

## 2. Cold Chain Considerations for Perishable Halal Goods

Perishable halal goods (meat, poultry, dairy, prepared foods, seafood) sit at the intersection of **food safety temperature control** and **halal integrity controls**. Both must hold simultaneously.

### 2.1 Temperature parameters

The halal-specific requirements do not change the physics - they inherit standard cold-chain numbers and add documentation:

- **Chilled:** fresh/chilled halal meat transported at core temperature around 0-5 C (halal transport guides state 0-5 C; EU Reg 853/2004 and SFDA guidance use a core of <=7 C with air temp ~6 C; Saudi chilled transport spec 1 C +/- 0.5 C for chilled meat).
- **Frozen:** -18 C or below for frozen products (SFDA: max transport temp -18 C; Brunei regulations: capable of maintaining -18 C, core not exceeding -12 C; frozen meat must be frozen at -18 to -35 C within 48 hours of slaughter, core no higher than -18 C).
- **Temperature tolerance:** fluctuations must stay within ~2 C; temperature must be recorded continuously (recording thermometers/thermographs) during storage and transit.
- **No thawing/refreezing:** frozen halal products must not thaw during loading, transit or unloading; loading time minimised; vehicles pre-cooled before loading (GCCA best practice: pre-cool to the coldest required temperature, 1-2 hours).
- **Cold-chain continuity:** cold chain must not break at handover points - container unloading into temperature-controlled docks, cross-dock transfers, airport cargo holds.

### 2.2 Halal-specific cold-chain issues

- **Segregation in the cold room.** Shared cold storage is common in 3PL facilities and is the riskiest configuration: 63% of halal SMEs in a Selangor study shared cold storage with non-halal products, and only 8.3% fully met temperature-control requirements (Miskam et al., IRJEMS). Where shared cold rooms are unavoidable, halal and non-halal must be physically separated with drip-containment; pork products require sealed packaging and dedicated shelves with drip trays.
- **Dedicated halal cold rooms** are preferred for halal meat and poultry. Cold-chain-certified providers (e.g., NL Cold Chain Network Malaysia, IGLO Port Klang, Eurofrigo Rotterdam) market JAKIM-certified cold stores with segregation, temperature monitoring and RFID.
- **Reefer containers.** Refrigerated ISO containers for chilled/frozen halal cargo must maintain consistent temperature with **data logging** throughout the voyage; seals intact from loading to unloading.
- **Temperature logs** that capture both food-safety compliance and cold-chain continuity from inbound receipt to outbound dispatch; importers' certifiers often request a cold-chain audit report with the import documentation.

### 2.3 Barriers in cold-chain halal logistics

Research (IIUM; IRJEMS; Terengganu agro-fisheries study) identifies recurring barriers: lack of temperature-controlled infrastructure (80% of small fishers cited lack of cold storage), shared cold storage without barriers, no real-time temperature monitoring, undocumented halal logistics training, and cost/awareness gaps among SMEs. Enforcement is weak for smaller operators. For the knowledge base: cold-chain halal logistics is a genuine capacity bottleneck and a differentiating service.

## 3. Freight Modes, Shipping Documents and Customs Clearance

### 3.1 Freight modes

- **Sea freight.** The dominant mode for halal trade volume. Containers (20ft/40ft ISO, reefer for chilled/frozen) are the standard unit. Key points: dedicated halal containers preferred for high-value cargo; shared containers require cleaning certification before loading halal goods; container seals must remain intact (tampering triggers inspection/rejection); the B/L must state halal status and list co-loaded goods. Port-level halal services exist: halal-certified warehouses at Northport and Penang Port (Malaysia), Tanjung Priok (Indonesia), Rotterdam (Eurofrigo cold store), Zeebrugge (Port of Zeebrugge certified 2012), Algeciras (Docks Logistics, 2019); Kuantan Port is implementing halal-compliant terminal operating procedures with JAKIM (pilot from Aug 2024). CMA CGM offers a ritual-cleansed (sertu) halal container service with certificate and special seal. There is **no IMO classification for halal cargo** and **no HS customs code for halal**, making identification of halal cargo in maritime supply chains difficult (Marco Tieman, LBB International).
- **Air freight.** Used for perishable and high-value halal goods. Certified air-cargo handlers: MASkargo/MAB Kargo (first cargo terminal operator classified as a halal cargo handler by JAKIM, 2019), Pos Aviation (halal cargo handling, ULD samak services per MS 2400-1, wrapping/sealing, segregation from general cargo), Nippon Express (halal-certified air cargo in Japan, halal-only roll boxes and rail containers). IATA Perishable Cargo Regulations (PCR) govern temperature-controlled air cargo handling; the Frankfurt University/Lufthansa Cargo "Halal Logistics in Air Freight" project mapped MS 2400 onto IATA's Master Operating Plan. Air handlers segregate halal ULDs, apply special handling labels, and use halal-certified ground equipment.
- **Road freight.** Halal transport by truck: dedicated halal vehicles preferred; shared vehicles permitted under most standards if sertu/samak cleansing is performed between loads and documented; no mixing of halal and non-halal on the same pallet/load carrier; bulkheads/partitions for mixed loads; halal seals; GPS + temperature telemetry for cold chain. Note the Islamic-ritual aspect: in some schools sertu for najis al-mughallazah (e.g., pig-derived contamination) requires washing 7 times, one of which with earth/clay solution.

### 3.2 Required shipping documents for halal products

Beyond standard trade documents (commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading/airway bill, certificate of origin), halal exports require religious-compliance documentation that varies by destination. Common set (HalalExpo, Global Trade Solution, Syrmex, SFDA, MOPH Qatar):

1. **Halal Certificate** - issued by a certification body **recognised by the importing country's halal authority**. Must be current, match the exact products/shipment, list the manufacturing facility; often batch- or shipment-specific. Accepted bodies differ per market (JAKIM-recognised list for Malaysia; BPJPH-recognised for Indonesia; ESMA-approved for UAE; SFDA-approved for Saudi Arabia; MOPH-authorised Islamic bodies for Qatar).
2. **Halal Slaughter Certificate** - for meat, poultry and raw gelatine of animal origin, issued by recognised halal centres (Saudi Halal Center-accredited; Qatar MOPH-authorised bodies). Saudi Arabia requires an additional attestation from the Saudi embassy in the exporting country.
3. **Health Certificate** - issued by the exporting country's competent authority, attesting fitness for human consumption.
4. **Certificate of Origin** - chamber/government-issued; must be legalised/apostilled for Saudi/GCC use.
5. **Certificate of Analysis (CoA)** - accredited laboratory analysis, per batch, for many processed foods.
6. **Product Registration / Import Permit** - SFDA product registration and import permit (permit number on shipping docs); UAE trade licence; Indonesian halal product registration.
7. **Temperature log** - increasingly required for frozen/chilled consignments.
8. **Phytosanitary / veterinary certificates** - for specific product categories.

Document consistency is critical: halal certificate vs invoice vs packing-list mismatches, expired certificates, unrecognised issuing bodies, and missing Arabic labels are the leading causes of port holds and import rejections.

### 3.3 Customs clearance specifics

- **GCC (example: Saudi Arabia).** Shipments inspected at Jeddah Islamic Port; clearing via the "Fasah" trade platform with SFDA-registered products and importers; origin certificate apostilled; halal slaughter certificate from Saudi-recognised centres; Arabic-first labelling with Hijri date where applicable; CIF-based commercial invoices. One export document is valid for one shipment only.
- **UAE.** DED trade licence, commercial invoice, chamber-approved certificate of origin, packing list with HS codes, import permit for restricted goods, original health certificate, and original halal slaughter certificate for meat/poultry.
- **Indonesia.** All food, beverages, cosmetics and pharmaceuticals entering must carry halal certification from a BPJPH-recognised body (post-JPH law); non-certified products face import rejection. Logistics services handling these goods must be halal-certified (PP 39/2021; draft 2025 decree).
- **Malaysia.** Importing halal products requires the certifying body to be on JAKIM's recognised list; products must comply with MS 1500:2019 (halal food standard) and MS 2400 (halal logistics); label placement per Malaysian regulations.

There is no harmonised HS code for halal, so customs officers identify halal consignments via the accompanying halal certificate, labels and freight documents; halal supply-chain stickers/coding on containers and freight documentation (e.g., Kuantan Port pilot) are emerging as a practical identification mechanism.

## 4. Segregation Practices During Storage and Transport

Segregation is the operational heart of halal logistics. The principle: keep halal goods physically separated from non-halal/najis goods so cross-contamination cannot occur - and make the segregation **real, maintained, and demonstrable**, not a line in a manual (getcommercialproperty.com).

### 4.1 Storage segregation

- **Dedicated vs shared facilities.** Halal goods may be stored in fully dedicated facilities (preferred for halal meat/poultry cold stores) or in clearly defined, physically separated zones within shared facilities. Halal and non-halal areas must be identified with signage, and access controlled.
- **Physical barriers.** Walls, partitions, bulkheads or dedicated pallet areas. A Selangor SME study found 58.3% of SMEs had inadequate physical barriers, relying on distance or plastic sheeting, which the standard does not accept as sufficient. Plastic wrap alone around an outer box is generally **not** accepted as sufficient physical separation by BPJPH guidance - dedicated separation is required.
- **Colour-coded equipment.** Dedicated pallets, bins, scoops, trolleys, forklifts for halal goods; 71.3% of SMEs did not use colour-coded halal-exclusive handling equipment (a compliance gap).
- **Stacking protocol.** Halal goods must be placed **above** non-halal goods in shared spaces to prevent drip contamination from liquids (e.g., meat juices, pork drips).
- **Quarantine zone.** Suspect/non-conforming products are quarantined separately; prohibited to rework or downgrade non-conforming products as halal.
- **Ritual cleansing (sertu/samak).** Where facilities, equipment or containers have been contaminated by moderate or heavy najis, a Shariah purification process is required before reverting to halal use (7 washes, one with clay/earth solution for najis al-mughallazah). Cleaning agents used must be halal-approved (no alcohol-based sanitisers).
- **Pest control.** Halal-compliant pest-management protocols only (no najis-based baits/sprays).

### 4.2 Transport segregation

- **No mixing on a load carrier.** Halal and non-halal goods are not mixed on the same pallet, in the same container, or in bulk shipments. For refrigerated shipments, there must be no mixing in the same compartment with severe najis (e.g., pork); ambient shipments must not mix halal and non-halal on a pallet/load carrier, with tertiary packaging protecting halal cargo.
- **Full loads vs groupage/consolidation.** Segregation must be physical, complete and documented for full loads, groupage (LCL) and consolidated air freight: sealed partitions/bulkheads or dedicated containers/pallets within a shared space.
- **Handling equipment.** Forklifts, trolleys and pallets must not carry halal then non-halal without cleaning; dedicated equipment preferred.
- **Vehicle discipline.** Drivers must not open sealed transportation equipment between authorised origin/destination warehouses; only the origin and destination warehouses may seal/unseal.
- **Terminal operations.** Cross-dock and transfer facilities must maintain halal segregation and handling procedures; port and airport terminals segregate halal ULDs/containers from general cargo (MASkargo, Pos Aviation, Kuantan Port pilot).
- **Sea-freight reality.** Mixed loading of halal and non-halal cargo in the same container is common today but is tightening under Indonesian rules (PP 42/2024 Article 22 allows mixing of non-animal processed products with halal processed products only with contamination-prevention guarantees and a declaration; fresh/frozen animal-origin products require separate facilities). Transshipped containers complicate verification, which the industry flags as a practical concern (JETRO Q&A with BPJPH).

### 4.3 The halal-compliant port / terminal

A halal-compliant port does not require the entire terminal to handle only halal cargo; it provides **segregation as a service**, like dangerous goods. Control points exist in: communication, transportation, warehousing, livestock holdings, and inspection. Ports and shipping lines are described as "the next wave" of halal logistics certification (HalalFocus, LBB International). A harmonised halal IMO code and HS code would materially improve identification of halal sea freight.
