# The End-to-End B2B Trade Process for Halal Goods

> Research ticket: `02-research-trade-process`
> Scope: sourcing journey, halal-specific considerations at each step, Incoterms 2020 basics, cross-border payment methods and risk profiles.

---

## Executive Summary

A B2B halal trade transaction follows a broadly standard international trade lifecycle — inquiry → quotation → negotiation → sample/approval → contract → payment → production → inspection → shipment → customs clearance — but with a halal integrity layer running alongside the commercial and logistics layers at every step. Halal compliance is enforced primarily through **documentation**: a shipment-specific Halal Certificate (or "batch certificate") issued by a certification body recognized by the importing country, plus declarations covering ingredient traceability, slaughter method (for meat), logistics segregation, and container cleanliness (Islamic Economy Academy; Halal Certification LLC).

Two frameworks govern the commercial mechanics. **Incoterms 2020** (11 ICC rules) define who pays for, manages, and bears risk of carriage, insurance, customs clearance and delivery — but they deliberately do **not** cover payment terms, title transfer, or which documents the buyer needs (ICC; U.S. ITA). **Payment methods** sit on a risk spectrum: cash-in-advance (best for the exporter, worst for the importer), letters of credit (bank-guaranteed, document-based, most balanced for large or first-time orders), documentary collections like documents against payment (moderate, bank-facilitated but not guaranteed), and open account (best for the importer, highest risk for the exporter) (U.S. ITA, "Methods of Payment").

For halal goods specifically, buyers and sellers should treat the halal certificate like a commercial document with the same strictness as the invoice or bill of lading: product descriptions must match word-for-word across the certificate, commercial invoice, packing list and bill of lading; certificates must be from a body accredited for the destination market (e.g., EIAC/GSO 2055-2 for the UAE, JAKIM/MS 1500 for Malaysia, BPJPH for Indonesia); and certificate validity must outlast the estimated arrival date (Halal Certification LLC; DHL). Halal logistics handoff — segregation from non-halal cargo, container cleanliness certificates, and chain-of-custody declarations — is increasingly a standard requirement in major halal import markets (Islamic Economy Academy; applyhalalcertification.com).

## 1. The Typical B2B Sourcing Journey

The classic export transaction proceeds through roughly ten stages. No two trades are identical, and several stages (negotiation, samples, inspection) can loop before the contract is fixed.

1. **Inquiry / request for quotation (RFQ).** The buyer (importer) sends a request describing product, specification, quantity, target price, and destination. For halal goods the RFQ should state the target market's certification requirement so the seller can price accordingly.
2. **Quotation.** The seller responds with a formal quotation or a **pro forma invoice** — a "quote in an invoice format" that includes seller/buyer details, itemized prices, weights and dimensions, discounts, **Incoterm**, terms of payment, estimated shipping date and validity date. Buyers typically need the pro forma to apply for import licenses, arrange pre-shipment inspection, or open a letter of credit (U.S. ITA, "Pro Forma Invoice"). Changes should not be made to a pro forma without the buyer's consent.
3. **Negotiation.** Price, Incoterm, payment terms, delivery dates, and halal documentation obligations are negotiated. Sample cost policy should be stated in the quotation to avoid later misunderstandings.
4. **Sample / pre-production approval.** The buyer approves samples (product sample, label/artwork, or both). For halal goods, sample approval should confirm the ingredient list, the halal mark on packaging/label, and the intended certification body and standard.
5. **Contract / purchase order.** A sales contract or purchase order fixes product description, quantities, prices, Incoterms, payment method, delivery schedule, governing law, and — critically for halal — **certification responsibility and liability for non-compliance** (Islamic Economy Academy).
6. **Payment.** Per the agreed method (see Section 4): typically a deposit/T/T before production, an L/C or documents-against-payment for the balance, or open account for established relationships.
7. **Production.** The supplier manufactures, segregates halal from non-halal production lines, and maintains auditable records. A Halal Assurance System (HAS) governs production, storage, and training so every step is repeatable and auditable (DHL).
8. **Inspection / quality control.** Either the buyer's own QC or a third-party pre-shipment inspection (PSI) firm (e.g., SGS, Pro QC, QIMA) verifies quality, quantity, and compliance before shipment (SGS; Pro QC). For halal, inspectors verify lot-level halal status, packaging/labeling, and container cleanliness in addition to general quality.
9. **Shipment & documentation.** Goods are packed, containerized, and shipped under the agreed Incoterm. The full export pack is assembled — commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading/airway bill, certificate of origin, health/veterinary certificates, insurance certificate, and the halal certificate(s) (Islamic Economy Academy).
10. **Customs clearance & delivery.** The importer clears the goods at destination customs, often against pre-registration on digital platforms (e.g., Saudi "Zad"/"Saber", Indonesia's BPJPH "Sihalal" system), then takes delivery (Halal Certification LLC).

## 2. Halal-Specific Considerations at Each Step

Halal trade adds a compliance layer that must be **documented, traceable, and recognized** by the importing country. Documentation is best understood in five categories: (1) halal & Shariah compliance, (2) commercial & trade, (3) logistics & supply chain, (4) regulatory & country-specific, and (5) risk mitigation & financial protection (Islamic Economy Academy).

### 2.1 Halal certificates accompanying shipments

The **Halal Certificate of Conformity** is the foundation document. Beyond the annual facility "scope certificate," most importing countries require a **shipment-specific batch or transaction certificate** that links the facility's certification to the specific lot numbers on the ship (Halal Certification LLC). Key requirements:

- Issued by a **certification body recognized by the destination country**, not a local mosque. Examples: UAE requires HCBs accredited by **EIAC under GSO 2055-2**; Malaysia requires JAKIM recognition under **MS 1500**; Indonesia's BPJPH recognizes foreign certifiers (via mutual recognition) but the certificate is issued/registered through its system (Halal Certification LLC; DHL).
- **Valid for the specific batch/shipment** and valid _after_ the estimated arrival date.
- For meat and poultry, a **Halal Slaughter Certificate** detailing slaughterhouse, slaughter date, slaughterman name, stunning method (if used), plus the official health/veterinary export certificate (e.g., USDA-FSIS Form 9060-5 in the U.S.).
- Backed by **ingredient and raw-material declarations**, including animal-derived inputs, alcohol/enzyme declarations, and cross-contamination controls (dedicated or sanitized lines, Halal Assurance System) (Islamic Economy Academy; DHL).

### 2.2 Certification documentation in L/C and contract terms

Halal documents are treated as **negotiable documents under a letter of credit**. Because an L/C is a "documentary" transaction — the bank pays only when the presented documents strictly match the L/C terms — the halal certificate, invoice, packing list and bill of lading must align exactly:

- The **contract/L/C should explicitly list the halal documents** required: e.g., "Halal Certificate of Conformity issued by [named body], covering batch numbers [X–Y], per GSO 2055-2." Document descriptions (product name, quantity, weight) must **match word-for-word** between the certificate and the commercial documents, or the bank may reject the presentation (Islamic Economy Academy; U.S. ITA, "Methods of Payment").
- The contract should assign **responsibility for certification and liability for non-compliance** (who pays for re-certification, re-export, or destruction if the cert is rejected) (Islamic Economy Academy).
- **Misalignment risk is expensive:** a container sitting at Jeddah or Port Klang can accrue USD 150–300/day in demurrage/storage, and a rejected, unrecognized certificate can trigger return freight or destruction costs (Halal Certification LLC).

### 2.3 Halal logistics handoff

The "halal chain" extends past the factory door. Requirements increasingly include:

- **Halal logistics declarations** confirming segregation from non-halal goods, dedicated/cleansed containers, and clean handling/storage (Islamic Economy Academy).
- **Container cleanliness certificates** (ISO 22000/HACCP-aligned) proving the container was not previously used for pork or alcohol without validated cleaning (Samak) (Halal Certification LLC).
- **Bill-of-lading notations** confirming halal handling to prevent cross-contamination — recommended for meat shipments, and required for import into Indonesia (Halal Certification LLC).
- **Cold-chain records** (temperature logs) for frozen/chilled goods, critical for insurance claims and spoilage disputes (Islamic Economy Academy).
- A **statement of compliance from the freight forwarder** for LCL (less-than-container-load) shipments ensuring halal pallets are physically segregated from non-halal cargo (Halal Certification LLC).

### 2.4 Regulatory & country-specific documents

Alongside halal docs, importers must assemble health certificates, phytosanitary/veterinary certificates, certificate of origin (often **embassy-legalized/attested** for MENA markets like Kuwait or Qatar), and any import permits/market authorization (Islamic Economy Academy; Halal Certification LLC). The certification framework the destination follows dictates the pack: **GSO 2055** (GCC: Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Oman, Bahrain), **SMIIC 1** (OIC/Turkey), or **JAKIM/MS 1500 and BPJPH** (Southeast Asia) (Halal Certification LLC).

## 3. Incoterms 2020 Basics

**Incoterms 2020** are 11 ICC rules that define the tasks, costs and risks of delivery between seller and buyer. They are **incorporated into the contract of sale** and do **not** cover contract price, method/timing of payment, title transfer, which documents the buyer needs for customs, or dispute resolution (U.S. ITA, "Know Your Incoterms"; ICC). Buyers and sellers should always reference the version explicitly (e.g., "FOB Incoterms 2020").

### Any mode of transport (7 rules)

| Rule                                   | Meaning                                                                                                                                                  | Risk/cost profile                                                                                 |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **EXW** (Ex Works)                     | Seller makes goods available at their premises                                                                                                           | **Minimum seller obligation** — buyer collects, handles export formalities, all carriage and risk |
| **FCA** (Free Carrier)                 | Seller delivers goods to a named place for the carrier; revised in 2020 to allow an **on-board bill of lading** when goods are sold FCA for sea carriage | Risk transfers at delivery to carrier; export clearance is the seller's duty                      |
| **CPT** (Carriage Paid To)             | Seller pays carriage to destination; risk transfers earlier, at first carrier                                                                            | Seller pays main carriage, buyer bears transit risk                                               |
| **CIP** (Carriage & Insurance Paid To) | Seller pays carriage + **higher-level insurance (Institute Cargo Clauses A)**                                                                            | Same as CPT plus mandatory enhanced insurance cover                                               |
| **DAP** (Delivered at Place)           | Seller delivers at destination place; **seller does not unload**                                                                                         | Risk transfers at destination; import duties not included                                         |
| **DPU** (Delivered at Place Unloaded)  | Like DAP but **seller unloads**; renamed from DAT in 2020                                                                                                | Same as DAP plus unloading obligation                                                             |
| **DDP** (Delivered Duty Paid)          | Seller delivers at destination **duty paid**, handling import formalities                                                                                | **Maximum seller obligation** — seller bears all costs/risk, including import duty                |

### Sea & inland waterway only (4 rules)

| Rule                                | Meaning                                                                                     | Risk/cost profile                                                                     |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **FAS** (Free Alongside Ship)       | Seller delivers alongside the vessel at the loading port                                    | Risk transfers when goods are placed alongside ship                                   |
| **FOB** (Free On Board)             | Seller delivers on board the vessel at the named loading port                               | Risk transfers once goods are on board; widely used for commodity and container trade |
| **CFR** (Cost and Freight)          | Seller pays cost and freight to destination port; **risk transfers on loading**             | Seller pays freight but bears no transit risk                                         |
| **CIF** (Cost, Insurance & Freight) | Like CFR plus **minimum insurance (Institute Cargo Clauses C)**; default in commodity trade | Seller pays freight + insurance; risk transfers on loading                            |

### Practical guidance

- **Key Incoterms 2020 changes**: FCA on-board B/L option; cost articles consolidated at A9/B9; **CIF keeps Institute Cargo Clauses (C)** as default while **CIP now requires Institute Cargo Clauses (A)**; DAT renamed DPU; clearer security-related obligations in carriage and export/import clearance (ICC).
- For halal trade, the Incoterm choice interacts with logistics obligations: under DDP the seller bears the full burden including import formalities in the destination country — where halal inspection and registration (e.g., Saudi Saber, Indonesia Sihalal) may be required before customs release (Halal Certification LLC).
- Older versions (2010 etc.) remain valid if both parties agree and the version is identified on documents (U.S. ITA).

## 4. Payment Methods in Cross-Border Halal Trade

The U.S. ITA defines five primary payment methods, ordered by risk from the exporter's perspective (U.S. ITA, "Methods of Payment"):

### 4.1 T/T / Telegraphic Transfer (cash in advance)

- **How it works:** The importer wires funds (often a deposit of 20–50%, with the balance on shipment or document presentation) before goods ship.
- **Risk profile:** **Zero non-payment risk for the exporter**; for the importer it is the least attractive option — unfavorable cash flow and concern that goods may never be sent. Requiring 100% advance can lose sales to competitors offering better terms (U.S. ITA). For halal buyers, T/T offers no documentary protection: no bank checks that the halal certificate matches the shipment.

### 4.2 Letter of Credit (L/C)

- **How it works:** A bank commits, on the buyer's behalf, to pay the exporter **provided the terms and conditions stated in the L/C are met, verified through presentation of required documents**. The buyer establishes credit and pays the bank for the service.
- **Risk profile:** One of the most secure instruments. **Protects the exporter** (bank pays on compliant documents) and **protects the buyer** (no payment obligation arises until the goods have shipped as promised). Ideal when the exporter lacks reliable credit information on the buyer but accepts the buyer's bank's creditworthiness. More costly and document-heavy than T/T (U.S. ITA; SSRN). For halal trade, the L/C is where halal certification terms are codified (see Section 2.2).

### 4.3 Documents Against Payment (D/P) / documentary collection (CAD)

- **How it works:** Under a documentary collection, the exporter's bank (remitting bank) sends the documents the buyer needs to the importer's bank (collecting bank), with instructions to release them **for payment at sight (documents against payment, D/P, effectively cash against documents, CAD) or on a specified date (documents against acceptance, D/A)**.
- **Risk profile:** **Bank-facilitated but not bank-guaranteed.** Banks act as intermediaries with **no verification process and limited recourse if the importer refuses to pay**. Cheaper than L/Cs; moderate protection — the exporter keeps control of documents (hence title) until payment, but the importer can still default at the payment deadline (U.S. ITA; World Bank). A common middle-ground used for repeat halal shipments where L/C costs are unwelcome but open account is too risky.

### 4.4 Escrow (third-party payment custody)

- **How it works:** A trusted third party holds payment and releases it when agreed conditions are met — increasingly used as a cash-in-advance option for smaller export transactions (U.S. ITA).
- **Risk profile:** Reduces buyer's fear of non-shipment compared with plain advance payment; conditions of release (including halal document verification) must be defined precisely. Less standardized in commodity/containerized trade than L/C; typically limited to smaller deals.

### 4.5 Open account / consignment (context)

- Open account: goods ship before payment is due (30/60/90 days). Best cash flow for the importer, **highest risk for the exporter**; often mitigated by export credit insurance. Consignment (payment only after the foreign distributor resells) is riskier still (U.S. ITA).

### Risk comparison (from exporter's perspective)

| Method       | Exporter risk                 | Importer risk | Halal-trade notes                                                              |
| ------------ | ----------------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| T/T advance  | Very low                      | High          | No documentary linkage to halal cert                                           |
| **L/C**      | Low (bank-guaranteed on docs) | Low           | **Codifies halal certificate/document requirements**; strict compliance needed |
| D/P / CAD    | Medium                        | Medium        | Bank-facilitated, no guarantee; docs (incl. halal cert) held until payment     |
| Escrow       | Low–Medium                    | Low–Medium    | Release conditions must include halal doc checks                               |
| Open account | High                          | Very low      | For established, trusted relationships                                         |

## 5. Key Takeaways for HalalNeo

1. The trade process is **ten stages long**, and halal compliance is a **parallel documentation layer** across all of them, not a single certificate.
2. The **halal certificate is a commercial document** under an L/C: its descriptions must match the invoice/packing list/BOL exactly, and the contract must name the issuing body and standard.
3. **Recognition is country-specific**: GSO/EIAC (GCC), JAKIM/MS 1500 (Malaysia), BPJPH (Indonesia), SMIIC (OIC). A cert valid in one market may be worthless in another.
4. **Logistics handoff matters**: segregation, container cleanliness, cold-chain and forwarder compliance statements are becoming standard.
5. **Payment choice is a risk trade**: L/C balances security and cost for first-time or large halal orders; T/T advances suit small/trusted deals; D/P/CAD sits in between; halal doc requirements should be embedded in the payment instrument.
