# Global Halal Certification Landscape

_Research ticket: 01 - halal certification. Status: draft. Date: 2026-08-10._

## Executive Summary

Halal certification is a **country-by-country patchwork**, not a single global scheme. Around **300 halal certification bodies (HCBs) exist worldwide, of which ~120 are officially registered** as active, holding formal accreditation or mutual recognition agreements with governments (PMC, 2026). Three bodies dominate: **JAKIM (Malaysia)**, **MUI/BPJPH (Indonesia)** and **MUIS (Singapore)** - collectively the most-recognised certifiers and the reference points most import authorities align with. Standards differ in meaningful ways (stunning, mechanical slaughter, seafood rules, labelling), so a certificate valid in one market does not guarantee acceptance in another. Market acceptance is governed by **recognition lists** - each importing authority (JAKIM, BPJPH, MUIS, SFDA/GAC, MOIAT) maintains a list of foreign HCBs whose certificates it accepts, often scoped by product category. For a B2B trade platform, the practical implications are: (1) certificate acceptance must be evaluated per target market, (2) certificate **scope, expiry and audit history** are the fields buyers actually need, and (3) mutual-recognition registration routes (e.g. Indonesia's SHLN) matter more than raw certification for cross-border trade.

## 1. Mainstream Certifying Bodies

### 1.1 JAKIM - Malaysia (the "gold standard")

The Department of Islamic Development Malaysia (JAKIM), a federal agency under the Prime Minister's Department, is the certifying and regulatory authority for halal in Malaysia (AHF; PMC). It develops halal standards, runs the national certification scheme (per the Malaysian Halal Certification Procedure Manual, 2020) and publishes the **list of Recognised Foreign Halal Certification Bodies (FHCB)**. The list was updated effective 29 August 2025 to **92 recognised foreign bodies** (Unit Halal, 2025); older figures cite ~85 HCBs from 47 countries (PMC, 2023) and ~88 bodies across 49 countries (ShariaCheck). **JAKIM recognition is the most sought-after mutual recognition in the industry** and is widely treated as a trust baseline. Certification is mandatory for food bearing the halal logo in Malaysia. Imported meat/poultry must additionally be approved by the Department of Veterinary Services (DVS) and JAKIM.

### 1.2 MUI / LPPOM MUI / BPJPH - Indonesia

Indonesia has a **three-actor model**: the **Halal Product Assurance Organizing Agency (BPJPH)** - the government authority under the Ministry of Religious Affairs (est. 2017) - issues halal certificates and manages recognition of foreign HCBs (called _LHLN_); the Indonesian Council of Ulama (**MUI**) is the supreme religious authority that issues the **fatwa** that validates halal status; and **LPPOM MUI** (now the halal inspection body / LPH) conducts technical inspections (PMC; AHF). Certification is **mandatory**: all food products since **October 2024**, cosmetics and pharmaceuticals by **October 2026** (ShariaCheck). Under Government Regulation **42/2024**, halal certificates can become **permanent** if ingredients and processes remain unchanged. Indonesia is the world's largest Muslim-majority market (~270M consumers); non-compliance risks product withdrawal and fines.

### 1.3 GAC & SFDA - Saudi Arabia / GCC

The **GCC Accreditation Center (GAC)**, based in Saudi Arabia, is the regional accreditation body that accredits halal certification bodies in accordance with **GSO 2055-2:2021** - effectively the gateway to Gulf markets (GAC; AHF). The **Saudi Food & Drug Authority (SFDA)** enforces halal compliance at import, requiring a **halal certificate and halal shipment certificate from an SFDA-approved body**, referencing GSO 2055-1 / SFDA.FD/GSO 2055-1 and GSO 993 for animal-derived products; conformity is processed through the **SABER** electronic platform (ShariaCheck; ChemLinked; BrGlobalTrade). Saudi Arabia is the largest halal market by value. The Saudi Accreditation Center (SAAC) also registers halal certification bodies against GSO 2055-2 (SAAC).

### 1.4 MUIS - Singapore

The Islamic Religious Council of Singapore (MUIS) is the **sole halal certification authority** under the Administration of Muslim Law Act (AMLA), Sections 88(1)-88(2) (PMC). Standards: **SS 590 / MUIS-HC-S001** (general guidelines) and **MUIS-HC-S002** (halal quality management / HalMQ). MUIS is one of the world's top three recognised bodies; certification is voluntary but required for any business displaying the halal mark. Because MUIS does not certify products made overseas, imports rely on **recognised Foreign Halal Certification Bodies (FHCB)** vetted against the Singapore MUIS Halal Standards (SMHS) under an **enhanced recognition framework** (updated 7 April 2026). FHCB recognition fees: $1,000 application + $120/person e-assessment + $2,000 recognition fee + $700/man-day audit (Tier 1). Older figures show 53 HCBs from 39 countries recognised by MUIS (PMC, 2023).

### 1.5 Emirates Authority / MOIAT & EIAC - UAE

UAE halal regulation previously sat with ESMA (Emirates Authority for Standardization and Metrology); the national scheme now runs under the **Ministry of Industry and Advanced Technology (MOIAT)**, which issues the **UAE National Halal Mark** and maintains the **registered halal certification bodies** list. Halal certification bodies must be accredited by the **Emirates International Accreditation Centre (EIAC)** against **UAE.S 2055-1 / GSO 2055-2**, and foreign bodies must be recognised by MOIAT (AHF; MOIAT; halalcenter.global). Mandatory for all food sold in the UAE; the UAE is the gateway to the wider GCC.

### 1.6 KMF - South Korea

The **Korea Muslim Federation (KMF)** is South Korea's halal certification body - one of few private-institution-based schemes in a Muslim-minority country. KMF certification is recognised by **JAKIM, MUI, MUIS, CICOT (Thailand) and ESMA/MOIAT (UAE)**, underpinning Korean K-food and K-beauty exports (ShariaCheck; korea-fmcg). The Korean government collaborates with KMF and is expanding halal certification for exports (2026).

### 1.7 Other notable bodies

- **GIMDES** (Turkey) - lead Turkish certifier/accreditation authority; World Halal Council member.
- **CICOT** (Thailand) - central national authority under the Thai government.
- **SANHA** (South Africa) - Africa's most recognised halal certifier.
- **IFANCA, AHF, ISA** (USA); **HMC / HCE** (UK / pan-Europe); **KazHalal** (Kazakhstan); **PSQCA/PHA** (Pakistan).
- Mutual-recognition networks: **World Halal Food Council (WHFC)** and **IHAF** aim to harmonise via reciprocal recognition between member HCBs.

### How the regimes differ

The academic literature (PMC 2023; PMC 2026) documents **material differences that matter for cross-border trade**:

- **Stunning**: permitted (with restrictions) in most regimes - Indonesia, SMIIC 1:2019 (electrical only, on severe resistance), Singapore, ASEAN Guideline, Malaysia, Thailand, Iran. **Pakistan and Brunei prohibit stunning entirely**; Brunei is the strictest ASEAN standard.
- **Mechanical slaughter**: permitted by GSO, ASEAN, Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, SMIIC and Iran; **prohibited in Pakistan and Brunei**.
- **Slaughterer**: must be a practicing Muslim in all regimes (SMIIC, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Iran, Pakistan); some regimes allow slaughterers from the "Semitic religions" (Ahl al-Kitab) - a key divergence.
- **Seafood**: most regimes treat all aquatic animals as halal except poisonous/harmful ones; **Pakistan restricts to scaled fish and shrimp**.
- **Insects**: generally only locusts permitted (Pakistan, Brunei, GSO, SMIIC); others vary.
- **Najis (impurity) definitions** and cleansing methods differ; Malaysia/Thailand/Singapore define three types, SMIIC uses two and prescribes purification methods.

Because there is no single halal standard or accreditation council, HCBs adopt **single or multiple standards** and seek accreditation from several bodies - which is why recognition, not the standard itself, drives market access.

## 3. Typical Certification Process, Costs & Timelines

### Process (application -> audit -> issuance -> renewal)

The standard lifecycle, as documented by certifiers (AHF; HalalCS; HCE) and confirmed by the country reviews (PMC 2026):

1. **Application** - submit company/product details, target markets, ingredient list; initial eligibility review.
2. **Agreement & fees** - sign certification agreement, pay deposit (often 50% non-refundable).
3. **Document evaluation** - review of ingredients, process flow, supplier halal status, Halal Assurance System manual (e.g. HAS 23000), facility documents.
4. **On-site audit** - physical inspection of facilities, production lines, segregation/sanitation, staff interviews; **lab testing** for porcine DNA/ethanol where animal-derived ingredients exist.
5. **Audit report & technical review** - non-conformities documented; corrective actions required (minor: 15-30 days; major: re-audit within 30-60 days).
6. **Shariah review / fatwa** - religious committee (e.g. MUI fatwa, JAKIM committee, MUIS HalMQ) validates compliance.
7. **Certificate issuance** - official certificate + logo licence; registration in the certifier's public database.
8. **Renewal / surveillance** - certificates typically valid **1-3 years** with annual surveillance audits; re-assessment required before expiry.

### Typical timelines

- **Simple facility, clean documentation: ~2 weeks**; typical end-to-end: **4-7 weeks** (AHF).
- Step-by-step example (HalalCS, ISO 17065-based): 1-2 days application, 2-3 days agreement, 5-7 days doc evaluation, 1-2 days on-site audit, 3-5 days report compilation, 3-5 days technical evaluation, 2-3 days Shariah/legal opinion, 1-2 days issuance.
- Export pathway (REACH24H): **direct official certification 2-3 months**; mutual-recognition/accredited-body route **1-2 months**.
- Indonesia: full BPJPH certification ~**28-43 working days**; foreign-certificate registration via **SHLN up to 30 working days** (BPJPH Decree 221/2025, effective 15 Dec 2025).

### Typical costs (USD)

- **Application/registration**: $500-$2,500 upfront (some bodies charge none).
- **On-site audit**: $1,000-$5,000 (plus separate travel); ~$700/man-day in Singapore's FHCB scheme.
- **Annual renewal**: $500-$3,000.
- **Lab testing**: $300-$1,500 when required.
- **Logo licensing**: separate flat fee per product or % of sales.
- **Total annual**: roughly **$250-$7,000**, scaling with number of facilities, product lines, ingredient complexity and target markets (AHF via LegalClarity). Indonesia SHLN government fee: IDR 800,000.
- Key cost drivers: number of facilities (each audited separately), ingredient complexity (emulsifiers, gelatin, enzymes = more tracing), and target-market recognition requirements.

## 4. What B2B Buyers Care About When Evaluating a Supplier's Certification

Synthesis from the recognition frameworks and platform guidance (ShariaCheck; MUIS; REACH24H; JAKIM/BPJPH practice):

1. **Certificate scope** - which products, product categories and facility sites the certificate covers. Recognition is often **category-by-category** (e.g. BPJPH recognises a foreign body only for specific categories); a certificate for one category does not cover another.
2. **Certifying body recognition in the target market** - not "is it halal-certified" but "is the issuing body on the importing authority's recognition list" (JAKIM FHCB list, BPJPH LHLN/SHLN list, MUIS FHCB list, SFDA approved bodies, MOIAT registered bodies). Verify **per market and per category**.
3. **Expiry date & validity** - certificates expire (typically 1-3 years) and must be renewed; Indonesia's GR 42/2024 allows permanent validity only if ingredients/processes are unchanged. Foreign-certificate registrations (e.g. SHLN) follow the underlying certificate's validity and must be updated on renewal.
4. **Audit reports / surveillance status** - annual surveillance audits and corrective-action history indicate ongoing compliance; request recent audit evidence and check for non-conformities.
5. **Accreditation of the certifier** - ISO/IEC 17065 accreditation, or GAC / EIAC / SAAC accreditation, adds confidence in the body's impartiality and technical competence.
6. **Verifiable certificate details** - certificate number, issuing body, scope, expiry; verifiable on the body's public database (e.g. JAKIM Verify Halal, BPJPH SiHalal portal, MUIS FHCB list) rather than the supplier's own PDF.
7. **Jurisdiction/regime alignment** - e.g. stunning or mechanical-slaughter policy differences may disqualify a certificate in a stricter market even if the body is otherwise recognised.
